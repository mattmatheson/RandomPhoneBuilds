Physical CD + DVD — Every Option, With Prices

A CD and a DVD in one jewel case, 25–50 copies. Here's every choice we'd have to make, what each one costs, and what I'd recommend. Researched 30 July 2026.

What we're actually making

One standard jewel case — the normal CD thickness, not a fat double — with a hinged tray inside that holds two discs. Disc one is the record. Disc two is a DVD of video. Front cover, a booklet or insert, and a printed back tray card.

This is a collector's item, not a distribution plan. Nobody is buying a CD in 2026 to listen to it. They're buying an object because they like the band. That should drive every decision below — it means spend where people can see it and don't spend where they can't.

One thing to know up front

At 25–50 copies, these are burned discs with printed faces, not pressed discs. Real pressing — the stamped aluminum thing a store-bought CD is — starts at 300–500 copies with a $1,000+ setup fee before you've made a single disc. Every option here is burned.

That's completely normal for a short-run band release and nobody at a show will ever know. The only practical difference: the discs will say "CD-R" in the clear inner ring, and a very old car stereo might be picky. Just don't let anyone print the word "pressed" on anything.

How many should we make?

Make 50. This is the one place the math is unambiguous.

That's not a typo. 50 copies costs about the same as 25. Short-run companies have floor pricing — there's a minimum charge to set up the print run whether you order 25 or 100, so the first 25 absorb almost all the cost and the next 25 are nearly free.

Going 25 → 50 doubles the product for roughly 10–20% more money. If we sell out of 50 we can always run more; if we don't, we've got merch for the next tour.

Option 2 — Printing the discs

What the disc faces look like. There are three real choices and they look genuinely different.

MethodLookCost (both discs, 50 sets)
Black thermal on silver Crisp black printed straight onto the silver disc face. Sharp, permanent, waterproof. Looks exactly like a 2000s promo CD. One color only. $63
Full color inkjet Full artwork on a white disc face. Photo-quality, any design. Slightly softer than thermal and the white base means a different feel than silver. $90–$120

Black thermal at $25 for 50 CDs is the single best value in this entire project, and I'd argue it's also the better look for this record — black on raw silver is period-correct and it'll sit right next to Charles's artwork instead of competing with it. Full color is available if the art demands it.

Please, no stick-on labels

They're the obvious cheap answer and they're a trap. A label that goes on even slightly off-center unbalances the disc, so it wobbles at speed — that means skipping, stuttering, and discs that stop playing. Paper labels also absorb humidity, warp, and tilt the disc over time, and some adhesives eat into the dye layer and kill the disc.

It's survivable on a CD. On a DVD, which spins faster and reads a tighter track, it's a known failure mode. We'd be shipping a collector's item with a built-in expiration date. Not worth saving $40.

Option 3 — The insert / booklet

The printed piece inside the front of the case. This is where people look, and it's where the record either feels like a real release or feels like a burned disc in a case.

There are two separate printed pieces and they're easy to mix up:

How many panels the booklet has is the main cost lever. This is what each one actually is:

InsertWhat you getCost (50 sets)
2-panel A single card. Front cover, and the back of it. Minimum viable. $45–$70
4-panel folded Pick Folds open. Front cover, two inside pages, back. Room for the tracklist, a photo, and credits. The standard. $60–$110
8-panel accordion Folds out four times. Room for a full lyric spread or a photo layout. $85–$140
12-page stapled booklet An actual little book. Every lyric, band photos, full credits, thank-yous. This is the collector move. $100–$170

Plus a tray card — the piece behind the discs that you see through the back of the case, with the tracklist on it. About $10–20 for 50, and it's not optional; without it you're looking at bare plastic.

Paper choices

  • 100lb gloss text — standard, shiny, what most CDs used. Cheaper.
  • 16pt card stock — noticeably thicker and stiffer. Feels more expensive in the hand. Slightly more.
  • Matte or gloss UV coating — matte reads modern and photographs better; gloss reads more 2003 and pops harder under lights.

For what it's worth: going from a 4-panel to a 12-page booklet costs roughly $40–60 more on the whole run and it's probably the highest-impact dollar in the project. It's the difference between "burned CD" and "we made a record."

Option 4 — Shrinkwrap

About $10–13 for 50 units. It's cheap and it matters more than it sounds — an unwrapped CD looks used, a wrapped one looks like product. Also stops the case popping open in a merch tub. Yes on this.

Why this doesn't actually work

There is no cheap way to print a disc face at home in 2026. Consumer printers that print directly onto discs have almost entirely left the US market. The current option is an Epson EcoTank ET-8500 or ET-8550 — $600 to $900. Buying a $600 printer to make 50 discs is worse than every other option on this page by a wide margin.

The alternative is stick-on labels, which we're not doing (see above), or hand-lettering 100 discs with a paint pen — which is only a good idea if we decide it's the aesthetic and lean all the way in.

There's also the burn time. 100 discs one at a time on a laptop is 5 to 8 hours of sitting there swapping discs. A duplicator tower fixes it for another $150–400.

Add it up and Option C is 12–20 hours of work plus equipment to save $150. Option B exists precisely because a company will print all 100 disc faces for about $65.

What we need from you before anything gets ordered

The manufacturing is the easy part and it's fast. These are the actual bottlenecks:

  1. What goes on the DVD. This is the real open question. Candidates: the Dreaming music video, the TV spot, Cobra live footage, behind-the-scenes from the video shoot. A 2000s bonus DVD ran 15–30 minutes — we should be honest about whether we have that much finished video yet, because it might mean waiting.
  2. Artwork for four pieces. Charles's album cover is one image. A finished package needs a booklet layout, a tray card, a CD face, and a DVD face. Either Charles extends the artwork or I lay it out from what exists. This is the longest lead item.
  3. A CD master. The EP is mastered for streaming. A CD wants 16-bit/44.1k files and ideally a proper pass on track gaps. For 50 units the streaming master is honestly fine — but worth asking Tate and Kris whether a CD master already exists.
  4. Booklet page count. 4-page or 12-page. About $40–60 apart, and it's the biggest visible difference in the whole package.
  5. Who's paying, and who owns the boxes. Band-funded, split, or something else. Needs deciding before I place an order.

One thing that isn't on anyone's price list: the DVD has to be authored. A disc that plays in an actual DVD player isn't a folder of video files — it needs menus and a specific disc structure. That's a few hours of work on my end and I'd rather name it now than have it surprise us later.

What I'd do

50 copies. Hybrid approach. Standard double jewel case, black thermal discs, 12-page booklet if the art can support it, shrinkwrapped, hand-numbered. Around $250–280 all in, sell at $25–30.

That's a real object, it pays for itself after nine sales, and it makes the mail-order gag in the TV spot literally true — which is a much better ad than a joke phone number pointing at nothing.

Say the word on the DVD contents and the booklet size and I'll start getting real quotes in.
